Lawns and Nature

Hello, I am very passionate about nature and our effects on it, and in this site I will talk about one of the largest causes of habitat destruction and biodiversity loss that no one mentions: the lawn.

Why lawns?

Many people who live in the suburbs are inclined to believe that where they live is closer to nature. Not like those pesky city dwellers that are surrounded by cement and smog. No, we live in the suburbs. Green, short grass as far as the eye can see. So natural! What an escape from the lifelessness of the city!!

WRONG!!!

Lawns are possibly the furthest you could get to true nature while also technically supporting life. One type of life, specifically.

Kentucky bluegrass Poa pratensis is a plant native to Europe, northern Asia and parts of northern Africa. It, as well as Bermuda Grass Cynodon dactylon and St. Augustinegrass Stenotaphrum secundatum are what most people think of as turfgrass. Now, these plants are not native to North America, but that is a whole other problem in itself. What I want to focus on, is how you find them in their natural range. In the wild, you do not see these neatly cut. You do not see them green year-round. And most crucially, you do not see them growing in a monoculture, or without insects.

What is the problem with this?

Lawns and nature

Almost all environments on Earth require multiple organisms (and even abiotic factors too, like the climate!) working in harmony to keep it healthy. Even taking away or changing just one of these factors can lead to environmental detriment. It is the reason why erosion exploded in Yellowstone after wolves were exterminated: without wolves regulating the elk population their numbers started to skyrocket, leading to overgrazing of grasses which help stabilize the soil with their roots (this, coincidentally, was also the main cause of the 1930's dust bowl). This balance is the same in the native range of our three dear grasses - caterpillars and other insects hide within and eat the leaves of the grass, which can later be eaten by birds which get eaten by bigger birds.

In an 'environment' like a lawn, no food web exists. Just one type of plant is completely unable to support a diverse ecosystem, let alone an ecosystem at all. Our obsession with keeping a pristine lawn devoid of 'weeds' (I hate this term so much) completely bars us from being able to sustain life in our yards. Not just that you're adding new (probably native!) plants to your property, but also the plethora of insects, birds and other life you will be helping as well.

This is only made worse by the fact we must keep our lawns under two inches tall at all times. Not even mentioning the air and noise pollution lawnmowers cause, having such short grass means insects are unable to live there. Now, I get that a lot of people find insects 'icky' and should not belong in neighborhoods. This is also a whole other problem in nature and entomology spheres and is not the topic of this page. What I will say at least, is that insects are the backbone of most ecosystems, and killing them all just because you find them scary will lead to a complete collapse of your local environment. Be normal about them.
